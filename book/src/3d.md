# 3D: meshes, materials and the sun

The same engine draws 3D: the same ECS, systems, clock and tools, with
lit meshes under the 2D layer. Turn it on in `Cargo.toml`:

```toml
[dependencies]
crabbyengine = { version = "1", features = ["3d"] }
```

Games that leave it off pay nothing for it. The C library (`crabby_ffi`)
always has it.

A 3D entity has a `Transform3D` (where it is, which way it faces, its
size) and a `Model` (which mesh to draw, and a tint). Here's a village of

## Space

3D space is right-handed with +Y up. The default camera looks along -Z.
Units are yours to choose; meters work well. A `Transform3D` has a
`position`, a `rotation` (a quaternion, `Quat`) and a `scale`, and is
built the same way as a 2D one:

```rust
Transform3D::at(vec3(0.0, 1.0, -5.0))
    .rotated(Quat::from_rotation_y(0.5))
    .scaled(2.0)
```

`t.looking_at(target)` turns it to face a point, and `t.forward()` is the
way it faces (its -Z axis). C++ has `crabby::transform3d_at(position,
rotation, scale)`, `crabby::looking_at`, `crabby::forward`, and `*` on
quaternions and vectors.

## Meshes and materials

A **material** says how a surface looks: its `color` (and an optional
`texture`), how rough it is (0 is a mirror, 1 is chalk), how `metallic`
(0 for most things, 1 for bare metal) and the light it gives off
(`emissive`). Lighting is physically based, as in glTF, so models made in
Blender look the same here.

A **mesh** is triangles with materials. There are three ways to get one:

- **Shapes**: `MeshData::cube(size)`, `plane(size)` and
  `sphere(radius, segments)`, then `ctx.create_mesh(&data, material)`.
- **Your own triangles**: fill a `MeshData` with positions, indices (three
  per triangle, counter-clockwise from the front) and optionally normals,
  texture coordinates and `colors` (one per corner, multiplying the
  material's color). Normals you leave out are computed.
  `create_mesh` checks that the data fits together.
- **glTF files**: `ctx.load_mesh("models/house.glb")` loads a `.glb` (or a
  `.gltf` with its files) from `assets/`, with every mesh in its scene,
  its materials with all their maps (base color, normal, occlusion,
  roughness and metalness, emissive), vertex colors, transparency and
  double-sidedness. Loading the same file again returns the same mesh.

Meshes and materials are cheap handles: make each one once, at startup,
and spawn as many models of it as you like. A `Model` tint
(`Model::new(mesh).with_color(c)`) multiplies the mesh's colors, so a
thousand cubes can come in a thousand colors from one mesh.

## Camera

`ctx.camera3d` is the 3D camera. It has a `position` and `rotation`, and
`look_at(target)` turns it to face a point. Its `fov_y` is the vertical
field of view in radians (60° by default), and `near` and `far` bound
what's drawn. `ctx.camera3d.screen_ray(mouse)` gives the ray under a
window point, for picking things with the mouse.

C++: `ctx.camera3d()` and `ctx.set_camera3d(c)` read and write it,
`ctx.camera3d_look_at(target)` turns it, and `ctx.screen_ray(point)`
returns the ray.

## Light and shadows

`ctx.lighting` holds the sun and the sky:

- `sun_direction`: which way sunlight travels. Straight down is noon;
  a low angle gives long shadows.
- `sun_color` and `sun_intensity`: the sun's color and brightness.
- `sky_color` and `ground_color`: soft light from above and below,
  scaled by `ambient_intensity`.
- `shadows` and `shadow_distance`: the sun casts soft shadows up to that
  far from the camera (60 units by default), fading out at the edge.
- `shadow_cascades` (1 to 4, default 4): how many shadow maps share that
  distance. Each covers a stretch further out than the one before, so
  shadows stay sharp near the camera even when they reach hundreds of
  units; neighbouring cascades blend where they meet. Fewer are cheaper:
  each one draws its shadow casters again.

The shadow maps follow the camera and move in whole texels, so shadow
edges stay still while the camera moves.

## Normal maps, ORM maps and glow

Besides `texture`, a `MaterialDesc` takes the maps that glTF models come
with:

- `normal_map`: surface detail (bumps, grooves, bricks) as tangent-space
  normals, OpenGL style. `normal_strength` scales it.
- `orm_map`: ambient occlusion in red, roughness in green and metallic in
  blue, multiplying `roughness` and `metallic`.
- `emissive_map`: multiplies `emissive`.

A texture gets mipmaps (smaller copies of itself) the first time a
material uses it, so it stays smooth far away and at a slant instead of
shimmering. Sprites drawing the same texture still use it at full size.

Materials are solid by default (`Blend::Opaque`), whatever their alpha.
`blend: Blend::Cutout` cuts out the pixels whose alpha is below 0.5
(leaves, fences, grass cards), in their shadows too. It costs more, as the
material's shader then also runs for hidden pixels and shadows, so keep it
for materials with holes. `blend: Blend::Transparent` makes a material
see-through by its alpha (glass, water). Transparent models are drawn after everything solid and
cast no shadows; they aren't sorted, so overlapping ones can show in the
wrong order. `double_sided: true` draws the back of each triangle too
(leaves, cloth). `ctx.update_material(material, desc)` changes a material
while the game runs.

## Custom shaders

A material can have its own shader, in [WGSL](https://www.w3.org/TR/WGSL/).
It doesn't have to do everything: it defines one or both of two functions,
and the engine does the rest (lighting, shadows, the depth prepass,
and transparency):

- `fn vertex(v: Vertex) -> Vertex` moves vertices, in world space: wind
  in grass, waves on water.
- `fn surface(s: Surface) -> Surface` changes each pixel: its `albedo`,
  `alpha`, `normal`, `roughness`, `metallic`, `occlusion` and `emissive`,
  filled in from the material first. It can read `s.world`, `s.uv`,
  `s.color` (the model's tint times the vertex color) and `s.view` (towards the camera).

A shader can read:

- `frame.time.x`: seconds since the game started (`frame.time.y`: since
  the last frame), `frame.camera.xyz`, `frame.sun_dir.xyz`,
  `frame.sun_color.rgb`, `frame.sky.rgb` and `frame.ground.rgb`;
- `material.custom[0]` to `[3]`: four `vec4`s from `MaterialDesc::custom`;
- `custom_texture0` and `custom_texture1` (from
  `MaterialDesc::custom_textures`), and the material's own textures,
  with `material_sampler`;
- `custom_array0` to `custom_array2` (`texture_2d_array<f32>`, from
  `MaterialDesc::custom_arrays`): texture arrays, many same-size images
  in one, picked by layer:
  `textureSample(custom_array0, material_sampler, s.uv, 2)`. Make one
  with `ctx.create_texture_array` or `ctx.load_texture_array`, as
  `TextureKind::Color` (painted colors) or `TextureKind::Data` (normal
  maps, masks and weights, read as stored);
- helpers: `hash2`, `noise2` (smooth value noise) and `fbm2` (five
  octaves of it).

Transparent materials can also see what is behind them, for water,
glass and heat haze. With `s.screen` (the pixel's position on screen, in
pixels):

- `scene_color(screen)` is the lit color of the opaque scene there;
- `scene_position(screen)` is the world position of the opaque surface
  there, and `scene_distance(screen)` its distance from the camera.

Offsetting `screen` bends what shows through (refraction), and comparing
`scene_distance(s.screen)` with the surface's own distance gives how
deep the water is. A frame with such a shader copies the scene once
before its transparent models are drawn; frames without one don't pay
for it.

`ctx.load_shader("shaders/water.wgsl")` loads one from `assets/`; with
hot reload on, saving the file changes the running game. A shader that
doesn't compile stops the game at load with the compiler's message,
pointing at the line; on reload, the message is printed and the old
shader stays. `cargo run --release --example shaders --features 3d` shows
a lake, grass in the wind and a pattern made in a shader.

## Sky and fog

`ctx.sky` draws a physical sky behind the 3D world, lit by the sun in
`ctx.lighting`: blue by day, orange and red when the sun is low, dark blue
with stars at night. The same air lights the scene: the sun turns orange
and dims as it sets, and the sky's light (bluish in the shade) replaces
`Lighting::sky_color` and `ground_color`. One world unit counts as a
meter.

- `atmosphere`: on by default. Off, the background is `ctx.clear`'s color
  and the light comes from `Lighting`'s colors.
- `haze`: dust and water in the air. More gives a paler sky, a bigger
  glow around the sun and redder sunsets.
- `sun_size` (degrees across), `stars` and `night_light` (moonlight and
  starlight, when the sun is down).
- `fog_density`, `fog_height` and `fog_falloff`: height fog, thickest at
  `fog_height` and thinning out above it. It takes the sky's color behind
  it (or `fog_color` without an atmosphere) and covers the sky near the
  horizon too.

`Sky::sun_direction_at(hour)` gives the sun's direction for a time of
day, for a day and night cycle: it rises in the east (+X) at 6, is
highest at noon and sets in the west at 18.

## Screen effects

A screen effect is a shader run over the whole lit 3D frame, for looks
that belong to the picture rather than to one surface: under water, heat
haze, a dream blur, sun shafts, outlines. It defines one function:

- `fn effect(p: Pixel) -> vec3<f32>` gives the new light at each pixel,
  from `p.color` (the light there so far, brighter than white where the
  scene is bright), `p.world` and `p.distance` (the nearest solid
  surface and how far it is from the camera), `p.sky` (nothing solid
  there), `p.screen` (in pixels) and `p.uv` (0 to 1 across the screen).

It can read other pixels with `scene_color(screen)` and
`scene_position(screen)`, and everything a material shader reads:
`frame`, the noise helpers, and `material.custom` and the textures of the
material it's given. Make one with `ctx.create_effect` or
`ctx.load_effect` (hot reloaded, like material shaders), and run it by
adding a `ScreenEffect` to `ctx.effects`. Effects run in that order, each
on what the one before left, after the transparent models and before
bloom and the post-processing below. `p.world` is the nearest *solid*
surface: transparent models don't count.

## Render targets

A render target is a texture a second camera draws the world into, each
frame, before the main view: for mirrors, water that reflects the hills,
a security camera's monitor, a portal. `ctx.create_render_target(w, h)`
makes one, and a `TargetCamera` in `ctx.target_cameras` draws into it.
Materials then use it like any other texture. It holds the lit scene with
its sky and fog, in the same HDR light as the frame before tone mapping
(so put it in `emissive`, not `albedo`, to show it as it is). Target
cameras use the main view's shadows and don't draw transparent models.

`TargetCamera::reflection(camera, target, level)` is the camera for a
level mirror at height `level`: `camera` mirrored below it, drawing only
what's above it (its `clip_plane`, which tilts the camera's near plane
onto the mirror, so it costs nothing). Read it at the screen position
flipped upside down, and update it every frame as the camera moves:

## Compute passes

A compute pass runs a shader on the GPU once per texel of a render
target, every frame, before anything is drawn: for things that live in a
texture and change over time, such as ripples, footprints and tyre tracks
in snow or mud, wet ground drying, spreading fire, or fog of war. It
defines one function:

- `fn compute(c: Cell) -> vec4<f32>` gives the texel's new value, from
  what it holds now (`c.value`, all 0 at first), where it is (`c.pixel`,
  `c.size`, and `c.uv` from 0 to 1) and its neighbours:
  `cell_at(pixel)` reads any texel as it was before this pass.

It also reads `frame` (`frame.time.y` is the frame's seconds) and the
values and textures of the material it's given, as screen effects do, so
the game steers it with `ctx.update_material`. Make one with
`ctx.create_compute` or `ctx.load_compute` (hot reloaded), and run it by
adding a `ComputePass` to `ctx.compute_passes`. Passes run in that order;
the same pass twice steps the simulation twice a frame. Materials read the
target as a texture, in the same frame:

## Post-processing

The 3D frame is lit in HDR: light can be brighter than white. On its way
to the screen, `ctx.post` decides how it looks:

- `exposure`: brightness in stops (+1 doubles it).
- `bloom` and `bloom_threshold`: light brighter than the threshold glows
  into its surroundings (0 turns bloom off).
- `temperature` and `tint`: white balance, cooler or warmer, greener or
  pinker.
- `contrast` and `saturation`: 1 keeps them as they are.
- `tonemap`: how bright light rolls off to white. `Tonemap::Aces` (the
  default) is film-like, `AgX` keeps the hue of bright colors,
  `Neutral` stays closest to the materials' own colors.
- `lut`: a color lookup table for grading in an image editor. Start from
  `Post::neutral_lut(32)`, save it as a PNG, paint over it, and load it
  with `ctx.load_texture`.
- `vignette`: darker corners.

## Terrain

Ground that goes on for kilometers is made of tiles: squares of
heightfield terrain, each from a grid of heights.
`ctx.create_terrain_tile(&heights, size, material)` makes one from n × n
heights, row after row along +Z, and you spawn it with a `Transform3D`
at its corner instead of a `Model`. Neighbouring tiles share their edge
heights, so a tile of 65 × 65 heights 2 m apart is 128 m wide and the
next one starts 128 m further on. A NaN height cuts a hole, for a cave
mouth or a tunnel.

Each tile is drawn more coarsely as the camera moves away: every height
up to `lod_distance` (by default the tile's size), then every 2nd height
to twice that, every 4th to four times that, and every 8th beyond. The
lighting doesn't jump between levels, because the normals always come
from every height. A short skirt hangs down around each tile's edge, so
there are no cracks where tiles of different detail meet.

The ground's look comes from `ctx.create_terrain_material`: up to 8
layers (grass, dirt, rock, ...) as one texture array, and a weight map
saying how much of each shows where. The weight map is a texture array of
`TextureKind::Data` too, laid over the ground from `weights_corner`
across `weights_size` world units: its first image's red, green, blue and
alpha are layers 0 to 3, and a second image holds layers 4 to 7. One
weight map can cover many tiles. The layers' textures are lined up with
the world, not with each tile, so they run on across tile edges, each
repeating every `layer_size` units. `normals` adds a normal map for each
layer.

On steep ground the layers are also laid on from the sides (triplanar
mapping), so cliffs don't stretch them. Flat ground skips the side
lookups, so the terrain costs no more than before.

`MeshData::heightfield(&heights, size, step)` gives the same triangles as
plain mesh data, for one detail level of your own.

To find the ground under a point on the CPU, to stand a tree, a
character or a camera on it, make a `Heightfield` from the same heights.
`height_at(x, z)`, counted from the tile's corner, follows the triangles
the tile is drawn with, so what it finds is exactly what you see. It
gives nothing off the tile or over a hole. `normal_at` gives the way the
ground faces there. `TerrainTileData::heightfield()` keeps one for a
streamed tile.

## Streaming big worlds

A world bigger than memory loads around the player and frees what's far
behind. The engine gives three pieces for this, and the game decides what
a "cell" holds: terrain, trees, houses, or all of them.

- `CellStreamer::new(cell_size, load_radius, keep_radius)` keeps a grid
  of square cells on the ground. Each frame, `cells.update(position,
  max_new)` works out which cells came within `load_radius` (at most
  `max_new` of them, nearest first, so the work spreads over frames) and
  which went beyond `keep_radius`. Read them with `to_load()` and
  `to_drop()`. The gap between the two radii stops a cell at the edge from
  loading and dropping over and over as the camera moves back and forth.
- `Job::spawn(|| ...)` runs work on a worker thread, and `job.take()`
  hands its result over once it's done, without waiting. Build the data
  there (heights, `MeshData`, decoded files) and keep GPU work on the
  game's thread. `TerrainTileData::new(&heights, size)` builds every
  detail level of a tile, and `ctx.upload_terrain_tile(&data, material)`
  is then the quick part. In C++, use `std::async` for the work.
- `ctx.free_mesh(mesh)` and `ctx.free_terrain_tile(tile)` give the GPU
  memory back. Despawn the entity that showed it too.

## Mixing with 2D

3D draws first and everything 2D draws on top: sprites, shapes, text, UI
and the inspector. A 3D game can have a 2D HUD with no extra work.
`ctx.clear(color)` sets the background behind both.

## Cost

Models with the same mesh are drawn together in one instanced draw call.
A depth prepass finds the nearest surface of every pixel first, so each
pixel is lit only once, however many models overlap it.
Models outside the camera's view are skipped, except when they cast a
shadow into it. Edges are smoothed with 4x multisampling.

On an M4 Max MacBook Pro, 100,000 spinning cubes with shadows run at
402 FPS, fullscreen with vsync off: 2.5 ms of CPU a frame, in 28 draw
calls. The same scene in Unity 6, made of GameObjects, runs at 54 FPS
([the comparison](performance.md#3d-crabby-vs-unity)). `ctx.models_drawn()` counts the
models drawn last frame, and `ctx.render_stats()` gives the frame's draw
calls and CPU time.
